1.2 Amplifiers as a Concept

In this section, we will look at the most fundamental signal-processing function, one that is implemented in some shape or form in almost every electronic system, namely, signal amplification. We will study the amplifier as a circuit building block. That is, we will consider its external characteristics for now and focus on operational amplifiers in Operational Amplifiers.

1.2.1 Signal Amplification

From a conceptual point of view the simplest signal-processing task is signal amplification. The need for amplification is required as transducers provide signals that are said to be “weak”, that is, in the microvolt (μV) or millivolt (mV) range and possessing minuscule energy. Such signals are too small for reliable processing, and processing is much easier if the signal magnitude is made larger. The functional block that accomplishes this task is the signal amplifier.

It is useful at this point to discuss the need for linearity in amplifiers. Special care needs to be taken in the amplification of a signal, so the information contained in the signal is not changed and no new information is introduced. Therefore when we feed the signal shown in Fig. 1.3 to an amplifier, we want to make sure the output signal of the amplifier to be an exact replica of the input, except of course for having larger magnitude.

Knowledge

In other words, the “wiggles” in the output waveform needs to be identical to those in the input waveform. Any change in waveform is considered to be distortion and is obviously undesirable.

An amplifier which preserves the details of the signal waveform is characterised by the relationship:

vo(t) = Avi(t) (1.4)

where vi(t) and vo(t) are the input and output signals, respectively, and A is a constant representing the magnitude of amplification, known as amplifier gain.

As can be seen, Eq. (1.4) is of a linear relationship, therefore the amplifier it describes is a linear amplifier. It should be trivial to reason that if the relationship between va and vi contains higher powers of vi, then the waveform of va will no longer be identical to that of vi.

Warning

The amplifier is then said to exhibit non-linear distortion.

The amplifiers we have discussed up to now are primarily intended to operate on very small input signals. Their primary purpose is to make the signal magnitude larger, and therefore they are thought of as voltage amplifiers. [14][14]The preamplifier in the home stereo system is an example of a voltage amplifier.

At this time we wish to mention another type of amplifier, namely, the power amplifier.

Such an amplifier may provide only a modest amount of voltage gain but substantial current gain. Therefore, while absorbing little power from the input signal source to which it is connected, often a pre-amplifier, it delivers large amounts of power to its load.

An example is found in the power amplifier of the home stereo system, whose purpose is to provide sufficient power to drive the loudspeaker, which is the amplifier load.

Here we should be observant that the loudspeaker is the output transducer of the stereo system; it converts the electric output signal of the system into an acoustic signal. A further appreciation of the need for linearity can be acquired by reflecting on the power amplifier. A linear power amplifier causes both soft and loud music passages to be reproduced without distortion.

1.2.3 Voltage Gain

A linear amplifier accepts an input signal vi(t) and provides at the output, across a load resistance Rt as shown in Fig. 1.7c, an output signal vO(t) that is a magnified replica of vI(t). The voltage gain of the amplifier is therefore defined by:

Voltage gain   (Av) = vO vI (1.5)

1.2.4 Power Gain and Current Gain

Warning

An amplifier increases the signal power. This is an important feature that distinguishes an amplifier from a transformer.

In the case of a transformer, while the voltage delivered to the load could be greater than the voltage feeding the input side, called the primary, the power delivered to the load [15] from the secondary side of the transformer. is less than or at most equal to the power supplied by the signal source.

On the other hand, an amplifier provides the load with power greater than that obtained from the signal source. That is, amplifiers have power gain.

The power gain of the amplifier in Fig. 1.7c is defined as:

Power gain = (Ap) ≡ load power (PL) input power (PI) = vOiO vIiI (1.6)

where iO is the current that the amplifier delivers to the load (RL), iO = vO/RL, and iI is the current the amplifier draws from the signal source. The current gain of the amplifier is defined as

Current gain (Ai) ≡ iO iI (1.7)

From Eq. (1.5) to Eq. (1.7) we note that:

Ap = AvAi (1.8)

1.2.5 Expressing Gain in Decibels

The amplifier gains defined previously are ratios of similarly dimensioned quantities. Therefore they will be expressed either as dimensionless numbers or, for emphasis,

  • as V/V for the voltage gain,

  • A/A for the current gain, and

  • W/W for the power gain.

Alternatively, for a number of reasons, some of them historic, electronics engineers express amplifier gain with a logarithmic measure. Specifically the voltage gain Av can be expressed as:

Voltage gain in decibels = 20log⁡ |Av|dB

and the current gain Ai can be expressed as:

Current gain in decibels = 20log⁡ |Ai|dB

Since power is related to voltage (or current) squared, the power gain Ap can be expressed in decibels as:

Power gain in decibels = 10logApdB

The absolute values of the voltage and current gains are used because in some cases Av or Ai will be a negative number.

Warning

A negative gain Av simply means that there is a 180◦ phase difference between input and output signals. It does NOT imply that the amplifier is attenuating the signal.

On the other hand, an amplifier whose voltage gain is, say, -20 dB is in fact attenuating the input signal by a factor of 10 (i.e., Av = 0.1 V/V).

1.2.7 Amplifier Saturation

From a practical point-of-view, the amplifier transfer characteristic remains linear over only a limited range of input and output voltages. For an amplifier operated from two power supplies the output voltage cannot exceed a specified positive limit and cannot decrease below a specified negative limit.

The resulting transfer characteristic is shown in Fig. 1.14, with the positive and negative saturation levels denoted L+ and L−, respectively. Each of the two saturation levels is usually within a fraction of a volt of the voltage of the corresponding power supply.

Obviously, to avoid distorting the output signal waveform, the input signal swing must be kept within the linear range of operation,

L− An ⩽ vI ⩽ L+ An
