3.2 Current Flow in Semiconductors

There are two (2) fundamentally different mechanisms for the movement of charge carriers and therefore for current flow in semiconductors:

  • drift, and

  • diffusion.

Let’s have a look at both these options in more detail and understand their effect on semiconductors.

3.2.1 Drift Current

When an electrical field (E) is established in a semiconductor crystal, holes are accelerated in the direction of E, and free electrons are accelerated in the direction opposite to that of E. This situation is illustrated in Fig. 3.3. The holes acquire a velocity vp-drift given by:

νp-drift = μpE (3.8)

where μn is a constant called the hole mobility where it represents the degree of ease by which holes move through the silicon crystal in response to the electrical field E.

Given the velocity has the units of cms−1 and E has the units of Vm−1, we see from Eq. (3.8) , the mobility (μp) needs to have the units of cm2V−1s−1.

For intrinsic [78] chemically pure silicon with no added impurities, meaning its electrical conductivity comes only from thermally generated electrons and holes. silicon:

μp = 480cm2V−1s−1.

The free electrons acquire a drift velocity (vn-drift) given by:

νn-drift = −μnE (3.9)

where the result is negative as the electrons move in the direction opposite to E. Here μn is the electron mobility. [79] for intrinsic silicon is about 1,350 × 103 cm2V−1s−1 Note that μn is about 2.5 times μn, signifying that electrons move with much greater ease through the silicon crystal than do holes.

Let’s now return to the single-crystal silicon bar shown in Fig. 3.3. Let the concentration of holes be p and that of free electrons n. We wish to calculate the current component due to the flow of holes.

Consider a plane perpendicular to the x-direction. In one second, the hole charge that crosses that plane will be (Aqpvp-drift) coulombs, where A is the cross-sectional area of the silicon bar and q is the magnitude of electron charge.

This then must be the hole component of the drift current flowing through the bar,

Ip = Aqpvp-drift (3.10)

Substituting for vp-drift from Eq. (3.8) , we obtain

Ip = AqpμpE

We are usually interested in the current density (Jp), which is the current per unit cross-sectional area (Am−2),

Jp = Ip A = qpμpE (3.11)

The current component due to the drift of free electrons can be found in a similar manner.

¶ Information 3.13: Directions Matter

Note that electrons drifting from right to left result in a current component from left to right. This is because of the convention of taking the direction of current flow as the direction of flow of positive charge and opposite to the direction of flow of negative charge.

Therefore,

In = −Aqnvn-drift

Substituting for vn-drift from Eq. (3.9) , we obtain the current density Jn = In/A as

Jn = qnμnE (3.12)

The total drift current density can now be found by summing Jp and Jn from Eq. (3.11) and Eq. (3.12),

J = Jn + Jn = q (pμn + nμn) E (3.13)

This relationship can be written as

J = σEorJ = E/ρ (3.14)

where the conductivity (σ) is given as:

σ = q (pμp + nμn) (3.15)

and the resistivity (ρ) is given by:

ρ ≡ 1 σ = 1 q (pμp + nμn) (3.16)

Please observe Eq. (3.14) is a form of Ohm’s law and can be written alternately as

ρ = E I (3.17)

Therefore the units of ρ are obtained from:

Vcm−1 Acm−2 = Ωcm

3.2.2 Diffusion Current

Carrier diffusion occurs when the density of charge carriers in a piece of semiconductor is NOT uniform.

For example, if by some mechanism the concentration of, say, holes, is made higher in one part of a piece of silicon than in another, then holes will diffuse from the region of high concentration to the region of low concentration. [80] Such a diffusion process is like similar if one drops a few ink drops in a water-filled tank.

The diffusion of charge carriers gives rise to a net flow of charge, or diffusion current.

As an example, consider the bar of silicon shown in Fig. 3.4a: By some unspecified process, we have arranged to inject holes into its left side. This continuous hole injection gives rise to and maintains a hole concentration profile such as that shown in Fig. 3.4b. This profile in turn causes holes to diffuse from left to right along the silicon bar, resulting in a hole current in the x direction. The magnitude of the current at any point is proportional to the slope of the concentration profile, or the concentration gradient, at that point,

Jp = −qDpdp(x) dx (3.18)

where Jp is the hole-current density (Acm−2), q is the magnitude of electron charge, Dp is a constant called the diffusion constant or diffusivity of holes; and p(x) is the hole concentration at point x.

Warning

The gradient dp/dx is negative, resulting in a positive current in the x-direction.

In the case of electron diffusion resulting from an electron concentration gradient, as shown in Fig. 3.5, a similar relationship applies, giving the electron-current density,

Jn = qDndn(x) dx (3.19)

where Dn is the diffusion constant or diffusivity of electrons. Please observe that a negative (dn/dx) gives rise to a negative current, a result of the convention that the positive direction of current is taken to be that of the flow of positive charge. [81] and opposite to that of the flow of negative charge.

For holes and electrons diffusing in intrinsic silicon, typical values for the diffusion constants are Dp = 12cm2s−1 and Dn = 35cm2s−1.

At this point the reader is probably wondering where the diffusion current in the silicon bar in Fig. 3.6(a) goes. A good question, as we are not showing how the right-side end of the bar is connected to the rest of the circuit. We will address this and related questions in detail in our discussion of the pn junction in later sections.

¶ Information 3.14: Relationship between D and μ

A simple yet powerful relationship ties the diffusion constant with the mobility:

Dn μn = Dp μp = V TwhereV T = kT q . (3.20)

The parameter V T is known as the thermal voltage. At room temperature, T ≃ 300 K and V T = 25,9 mV.

This relationship is known as the Einstein relationship. [82] The Einstein relation connects a particle’s diffusion coefficient to its mobility, linking random thermal motion with how easily it moves through a medium under a force.
